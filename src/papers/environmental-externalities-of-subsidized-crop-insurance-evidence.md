Subsidized crop insurance protects farmers against agricultural risk, but it can also distort production decisions and generate unintended environmental consequences. We exploit China's staggered rollout of full cost insurance (FCI) and planting revenue insurance (PRI) for rice, maize, and wheat as a quasi-natural experiment. We develop a model in which a risk-averse farmer applies fewer inputs because output is sold at an uncertain price. FCI insures yield losses but leaves price risk uninsured, which lowers input use and nutrient runoff under any distribution of shocks. PRI insures both yield and price shocks and raises input use when its payouts arise mainly from price declines. Using a monthly county panel of surface water quality, we find that total nitrogen and total phosphorus concentrations decline on average after the reform, but these averages mask sharp differences across contracts. FCI reduces fertilizer use and improves water quality, with no meaningful reduction in grain yield; the water quality improvements are larger in areas with greater historical climate risk and insurance claim rates. PRI availability is associated with heavier fertilizer application and higher grain yield, reversing the water quality gains from FCI. At the central calibration, welfare is highest under FCI as its environmental benefit outweighs its production cost; revenue protection becomes preferable when the social value of grain or farmers' risk aversion is sufficiently high. The environmental and welfare effects of crop insurance therefore depend on the risks the contract protects.
